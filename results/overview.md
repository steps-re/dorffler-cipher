Between 1682 and 1690, the dispatches of Simon Dörffler, the Swedish resident at the Polish court, to King Charles XI of Sweden document the politics, diplomacy, and military affairs of the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth and its neighbors (19/100 p0276, 19/100 p0302, 19/101 p0223, 19/102 p0474). A central theme throughout the correspondence is Poland's war against the Ottoman Empire and the Crimean Tatars, alongside the Hungarian rebellion led by Imre Thököly and Poland's shifting alliance with the Holy Roman Emperor (19/100 p0283, 19/101 p0212, 19/101 p0357, 19/103 p0306). Dörffler writes extensively about the turbulent sessions of the Polish Sejm, where rival domestic factions and foreign envoys from France, Austria, and Brandenburg competed for influence through bribes, marriage schemes, and political intrigue (19/100 p0326, 19/101 p0277, 19/102 p0483, 19/103 p0017). Across these years, Dörffler worked to protect Swedish interests concerning Livonia, Courland, and Ducal Prussia while repeatedly pleading with Stockholm for his overdue salary and funds to pay his creditors and maintain his position at court (19/100 p0279, 19/100 p0295, 19/101 p0320, 19/102 p0421).

In volume 19/100, Dörffler reports on the escalating conflict in Hungary involving Thököly and the Ottomans, Poland's reluctance to break with Moscow, and negotiations for a military alliance between the Polish King and the Emperor (19/100 p0277, 19/100 p0279, 19/100 p0283, 19/100 p0329). Communicating closely with the Imperial envoy Baron Zierowski, Dörffler sought to safeguard Swedish interests and keep the new Polish-Imperial treaty open for Sweden to join, while defending the Livonian reduction and monitoring threats in Lithuania (19/100 p0295, 19/100 p0314, 19/100 p0331, 19/100 p0332). At the chaotic Sejm in Warsaw, Brandenburg partisans and French diplomats such as Du Vernay, the Marquis de Béthune, and the Marquis de Vitry distributed bribes and worked to disrupt the proceedings and prevent an Imperial alliance (19/100 p0279, 19/100 p0281, 19/100 p0324, 19/100 p0350). Factional tensions peaked when intercepted correspondence exposed Crown Treasurer Morsztyn's secret collusion with France against the Polish King, leading to outrage in the Senate, Morsztyn's forced submission and resignation, and the successful signing of the Polish-Imperial alliance treaty (19/100 p0326, 19/100 p0333, 19/100 p0338, 19/100 p0381). As the Turks besieged Vienna, Dörffler held private talks with the Polish King at Wilanów regarding a plan to march Swedish troops from Livonia through Lithuania into Ducal Prussia to divert Brandenburg forces under the pretext of aiding against the Ottomans, while also addressing Brandenburg postal disputes and settlements over the Radziwiłł estates (19/100 p0352, 19/100 p0392, 19/100 p0393, 19/100 p0403).

Volume 19/101 covers ongoing military operations against Ottoman, Tatar, and Kuruc forces in Hungary, Ukraine, and Wallachia, as well as turbulent Cossack affairs that saw the execution of their commander Kunicki and the installation of his successor Mohyła (19/101 p0067, 19/101 p0084, 19/101 p0086, 19/101 p0100). At the Polish court, Dörffler requested instructions on how to handle the French envoy de Béthune and used royal funds to reward Polish officials who helped thwart the Danish minister du Cros, who sought Polish mediation between the Empire and France (19/101 p0086, 19/101 p0204, 19/101 p0219, 19/101 p0276). While relations between Poland and Austria cooled over an Imperial marriage alliance with Bavaria, the Polish Queen planned to marry Prince Jakub to a French princess, prompting proposals for a counter-alliance with the Emperor, Brandenburg, and Courland (19/101 p0212, 19/101 p0219, 19/101 p0277). Meanwhile, landowners and nobles in Pilten and Courland grew restless over legal and religious encroachments, leading several to secretly approach Dörffler about seeking the protection of the Swedish King (19/101 p0320). The later part of the volume records extensive Sejm debates over tax tariffs, military funding, winter quarters, treasury reforms, and severe inflation caused by a poor harvest and the widespread counterfeiting of copper shillings (19/101 p0339, 19/101 p0453, 19/101 p0535, 19/101 p0547).

In volume 19/102, reports describe Polish military clashes with Tatars and Turks in Ukraine and Wallachia alongside Muscovite campaigns toward Perekop and subsequent peace negotiations between Moscow and the Tatar Khan (19/102 p0099, 19/102 p0173, 19/102 p0487, 19/102 p0540). Amid ongoing disputes over Pilten and grievances against Ducal Prussia, delegates from Courland secretly indicated their willingness to separate from Poland and recognize the Swedish King as their liege lord in exchange for protection, while in Warsaw a Polish castellan's seizure of Danzig merchants' goods highlighted growing lawlessness (19/102 p0328, 19/102 p0470). Political turmoil erupted at the Sejm over the marriage of Princess Radziwiłł to the Count Palatine of Neuburg and the control of her Lithuanian estates, as well as the Polish Queen's alleged diversion of royal funds from Marienburg to support her political intrigues (19/102 p0418, 19/102 p0467, 19/102 p0502, 19/102 p0503). The Sejm collapsed after a violent clash in church involving the Bishop of Vilnius led to an ecclesiastical interdict, and as the Polish King vowed to ruin the opposing Sapieha family, Imperial, Swedish, and Brandenburg diplomats discussed financially backing Hetman Sapieha to preserve Polish liberties (19/102 p0474, 19/102 p0476, 19/102 p0482, 19/102 p0484). By the end of the volume, the Polish court shifted back toward Austria through marriage negotiations for Prince Jakub, who warned the French minister Gravelle to leave the country, while Dörffler also reported on the papal election of Cardinal Ottobono, a complaint over French fabrics seized at Stade, and his own severe financial hardships (19/102 p0583, 19/102 p0588, 19/102 p0596, 19/102 p0598).

Volume 19/103 focuses on a prolonged and chaotic Sejm in which conspiracies involving the Crown Hetman, the Sapiehas, the Crown Marshal, and deputies corrupted by French money prevented any legislative conclusion (19/103 p0017, 19/103 p0033, 19/103 p0036). Disorder in Warsaw was marked by daily killings, a deliberate fire in the Royal Castle, the theft of gold and jewels from a prayer book in the King's cabinet by a young nobleman, and the theft of the King's tracking dog (19/103 p0033, 19/103 p0034). To resolve tax disputes and maintain a standing army year-round, the Bishop of Kraków proposed an excise tax on beer, brandy, wine, and mead across Polish towns and villages, though foreign ministers feared such a measure would allow the King to govern without Diets (19/103 p0035, 19/103 p0036). Meanwhile, French diplomats faced trouble over exposed intrigues as Gravelle fled to a monastery after failing to produce credentials, while Polish and Imperial commissioners drafted the marriage contract between Prince Jakub and the Princess of the Palatinate (19/103 p0010, 19/103 p0017, 19/103 p0064). Following the rupture of the Diet, the Polish King decided at a Senate Council in Grodno to continue the war against the Ottomans even as Janissaries and Tatar forces under the Nuradin Sultan devastated Volhynia and caused great fear in Lviv (19/103 p0306, 19/103 p0403).
